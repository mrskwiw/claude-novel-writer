# CHAPTER 1: THE SALON'S SHADOW

Émilie arranged playing cards in precise fans across the green baize tables. The salon's air hung heavy tonight, like the pressure before a storm.

"Three stacks of hundred-livre chips for the north table," her mother called. "And make certain Citizen Billaud-Varenne's usual place is properly prepared."

"Yes, Maman." The mother-of-pearl counters glinted like fish scales beneath her fingers. The first guests' voices carried from the entrance hall.

"—absolutely insists the Assembly must move quickly—" A nobleman's cultured tones cut off.

"—three more disappeared from Saint-Antoine this week—"

"—the old wards must be maintained, regardless of cost—" This whisper echoed from the walls themselves.

The salon filled with her mother's careful mixture of old nobility and new power. Maman's greetings marked each arrival's status: a deep curtsey for the duc d'Orléans, an embracing kiss for the marquise de Condorcet, a respectful but distant nod for Billaud-Varenne in his plain black coat.

The comte de Montfaucon entered precisely at eight, as he had every Thursday for the past decade. Her mother intercepted him at the threshold.

"Your usual table is prepared, Monsieur le Comte. The Meissen porcelain cup, warmed cognac, and of course—" The alcove table waited in shadows cast by the elaborate rokoko pilaster. The comte's pale face disappeared into the dimness without a word.

"—blood will tell, mark my words—"

"—the people grow restless, and hungry—"

"—certain obligations cannot be abandoned, even in these times—"

A chill draft swirled around Émilie's ankles as the marquis de Saint-Huruge entered. The candle flames bent sideways despite the closed windows. In the pier glass, his face transformed—hollow, skull-like—then snapped back to normal.

"—the old families are weakening—"

"—did you see how the de Montmorency boy aged overnight?—"

"—some prices must be paid in full—"

"Émilie, dear, fetch more wine for Monsieur de Robespierre's table." Her mother's voice held that particular tension. Émilie threaded between tables where politics and fortunes mingled.

The wine cabinet's mirrored back reflected a salon slightly askew from reality. Something moved behind the glass.

"—the sealed chambers in Versailles are leaking—"

"—servants aging decades in a night—"

"—the old bindings require noble blood—"

The comtesse de Noailles's triumphant laugh pierced the air, her painted fan fluttering. In the mirror, the laugh lingered, stretching the reflection's face into something inhuman.

Émilie reached for a bottle of her mother's best Bordeaux. Her reflection stared back with black, pupilless eyes. The wine bottle trembled against her palm.

The gilded chairs' ornate carvings writhed in her peripheral vision. A cherub's carved face had transformed into a skull when she wasn't looking.

"Is everything all right, my dear?" Her mother's cool hand touched her wrist. The mirror showed empty space where Maman stood.

"—the barriers grow thinner each day—"

"—the revolution itself may break what's left of the seals—"

Her heart hammered against her ribs. "The wine, for Monsieur de Robespierre's table."

"Good girl." Her mother's fingers squeezed. "And after that, check the private salon. We have some special guests arriving later."

Above, the crystal chandelier held not candlelight but shapes moving in darkness, pressing against the facets like moths against a lamp. A thin crack spread across one crystal, silent as a spider's web.

Émilie crossed the room, clinging to familiar sensations: the wine bottle's weight, clinking coins, murmured conversations. The mirror's gaze followed. The furniture turned to watch her pass. At Robespierre's table, his shadow stretched across the wall with impossible fingers, reaching toward her through the candlelight.

---

Sunset painted the salon in deepening shades of amber and crimson, the light taking on an almost liquid quality as it pooled in the crystal glasses and gilded frames. As the first nobles arrived, their jewels caught and scattered the dying light in strange patterns across the walls. The mirrors seemed to drink in these refractions, holding them a moment too long before releasing them back into the dimming room. Each flash of light left afterimages that twisted into shapes just at the edge of recognition before fading away.

The Comte de Sartine's son Charles arrived in a dark blue coat with silver buttons that gleamed like mercury in the fading light. He moved with the easy grace of the nobility. When one of the larger mirrors tilted unexpectedly, the heavy frame groaning against its moorings with a sound like distant thunder, he stepped forward to help her adjust it.

"These old frames can be troublesome," he said softly, his fingers brushing hers on the gilt edge. The touch sent an unexpected warmth through her hand, at odds with the chill emanating from the mirror's surface. "Sometimes they seem to have a will of their own." His voice carried the weight of knowledge beyond his years.

Before she could respond, her mother materialized beside them like a shade stepping out of the deepening shadows. "Monsieur de Sartine, your usual table awaits." Madame de Sainte-Amaranthe's voice was honey-smooth, but her hand was firm as she guided Charles away from the mirror. "Émilie, ensure the Duchesse is seated facing east." The precision of her mother's instructions carried an urgency that seemed to vibrate in the air like the last note of a struck crystal glass.

The evening progressed with the precision of a court dance, each movement carefully choreographed to an unheard rhythm. As more guests arrived in their silks and brocades, Émilie observed her mother's careful orchestration. Certain nobles were positioned facing mirrors, while others were subtly directed to seats that kept their backs to the reflecting glass. Each placement seemed to follow rules Émilie couldn't quite grasp, like a game whose strategy remained just beyond her understanding.

A group of prominent merchants entered, their tricolor cockades bright against their dark coats, bringing with them the charged atmosphere of the streets. They laughed and joked about the latest Assembly reforms, their confidence filling the room like fresh air in a long-sealed chamber. The nobles tensed at their arrival, but the merchants seemed not to notice, too caught up in their own rising fortunes.

The Marquis de Lafayette arrived last, his presence drawing fresh whispers about the latest debates in the National Assembly. Lafayette avoided his reflection entirely as he took his designated seat, though Émilie caught him casting a single, sharp glance at the largest mirror before turning resolutely away. His hand, she noticed, rested near the pocket where gentlemen typically kept their dueling pistols, though what threat he anticipated remained unclear.

The reflections deepened as night crept in, each mirror becoming a window into a darker version of the salon. When Émilie passed the one Charles had helped her adjust, she could have sworn her reflection turned its head to watch her go. In the glass, the candlelight caught her eyes strangely, making them appear almost black, like windows into some bottomless depth. She hurried past, but not before noticing that while her reflection's head had turned to follow her movement, its eyes remained fixed straight ahead, gazing at something beyond the boundaries of the mirror's frame. She shivered, wondering at herself.

Around her, the usual sounds of the gambling salon—the snap of cards, the clink of coins, the murmur of conversation—seemed muffled, as though reaching her through layers of thick glass. Only the mirrors appeared truly alive, their surfaces rippling occasionally like dark water disturbed by something moving far below. The revolutionary merchants continued their lively debates, their voices carrying dreams of a new order, while behind them, in the deepening glass, shadows gathered that remembered orders far older than the monarchy they sought to overthrow.

---

The cellar stairs creaked beneath Émilie's feet as she descended, candlelight casting her shadow against the curved stone walls. The evening above required more wine, and as her mother's daughter, such errands fell to her rather than the servants. The air grew colder with each step, carrying the musty sweetness of aged wood and cork, underlaid with something older and less pleasant.

At the bottom of the stairs, shadows pooled thick as ink in the corners. Her candle's flame stretched unnaturally tall, as though reaching for something in the darkness. The wine racks lined the walls in neat rows, bottles gleaming dully like sleeping eyes. As she moved toward the Bordeaux section, a sudden draft stirred her skirts, though there were no windows or doors to admit such a breeze.

The cold air seemed to emanate from the wall itself. Émilie ran her free hand along the rough stone, feeling for the source. Her fingers caught on a groove so subtle she might have missed it had she not been searching. It was perfectly straight, too regular to be natural, running vertically from floor to ceiling. Another crossed it horizontally at waist height, forming the barest suggestion of a doorframe.

Her heart quickened as she traced the intersection. Something clicked softly within the wall, and a section of stone shifted under her touch. The hidden panel swung inward with well-oiled silence, revealing a narrow passage. The draft carried a metallic scent now, like old coins or fresh blood.

Beyond the opening, a cramped corridor led toward the salon above. Émilie lifted her candle higher, revealing a series of small openings set into the passage wall at eye level. She moved closer to the nearest one, drawn by a faint golden glow. When she peered through, she found herself looking into the salon through the very mirror she and Charles had adjusted earlier.

The view encompassed the entire main room, but something was wrong with the perspective. The reflection she saw didn't match the normal mirror image—it showed angles that shouldn't have been possible, as though the glass opened into a space that existed somewhere between reality and reflection. Through this impossible view, she watched her mother direct the nobles with newfound significance. Each placement, each careful arrangement of seats and tables now seemed part of a pattern she could almost grasp.

A sudden scraping sound echoed from deeper in the passage. Émilie froze, the candle trembling in her hand. The noise came again, closer this time. Her pulse quickened as she glanced between the open panel and the darkness beyond her candle's reach, where something moved with deliberate purpose.

The flame wavered, bending toward the thing in the darkness as though pulled by an invisible force. Through the peephole, she could still see the salon, could even make out Charles at his table, so close yet impossibly far away. In the mirror's reflection, a shadow began to coalesce behind her own image, something darker and denser than the surrounding darkness.

Émilie didn't wait to see more. She backed away from the peephole, holding the candle before her like a shield. A dragging sound grew behind her, and she turned and fled through the open panel, her slippers silent on the cellar floor. Only when she had reached the cellar again did she dare to look back. A rectangle of absolute darkness loomed behind her. The scraping came again, and she slammed the door shut.

Her hands shook as she selected two bottles of Bordeaux, and mounted the stairs.

---

The salon's brilliance seemed different to Émilie now, every reflection holding secrets. She moved between the tables with the wine bottles, hands still trembling slightly from her cellar discovery. The mirrors watched her progress with patient attention, their depths shifting like dark water whenever she turned her head.

Charles caught her eye as she passed his table, his expression changing subtly as he noted her pallor. "Mademoiselle," he said, rising to take one of the bottles from her hands. "The cellar air doesn't agree with you." His fingers brushed hers again, deliberate this time, and she felt a warmth that seemed to push back against the lingering cellar chill.

"The bottles were difficult to locate," she managed, aware of her mother watching from across the room. In the nearest mirror, she could see Madame de Sainte-Amaranthe's reflection standing at a slightly different angle than her physical form, as though the two were slowly diverging.

The gambling reached its height around them. Cards snapped against green baize, coins clinked, and the revolutionary merchants grew louder with each glass of wine. Their tricolor cockades seemed to blur in the mirrors, leaving trails of color like oil on water. One particularly vocal lawyer was holding forth about the Assembly's latest declarations, his gestures becoming more emphatic with each point.

"Soon there will be no more privileges," he declared, "no more ancient rights!" The mirrors trembled almost imperceptibly at his words, and Émilie saw several nobles stiffen. Charles's hand moved to his coat pocket, where something that caught the light—silver, perhaps, or older metal.

"Some rights," he said quietly, meant for Émilie alone, "are ancient for good reason." His reflection watched them both with eyes that seemed darker than they should be, holding knowledge that his living face carefully concealed.

The lawyer's next declaration was cut short by a sudden gust of cold air that swept through the salon, though all the windows remained firmly shut. The candles flickered in unison, their flames bending toward the mirrors as though drawn by an unseen force. In that moment of wavering light, Émilie saw things moving in the glass—shapes that had no source in the room, shadows that twisted in ways that made her eyes hurt to follow.

Her mother appeared beside them, her smile fixed but her eyes sharp with worry. "Monsieur de Sartine," she said, "I believe your father's table requires your presence." She squeezed Émilie's arm as Charles bowed and withdrew, a gesture that felt like both warning and reassurance.

Through the peephole in the cellar, Émilie had glimpsed her mother's careful choreography of the salon. Now she saw the pattern continuing, as certain nobles were subtly guided away from the mirrors while others were positioned before them like pieces in some incomprehensible game. The revolutionary merchants remained oblivious, caught up in their dreams of a new order, while around them an older power stirred in the depths of the mirrors, watching their bold declarations with growing interest.

A burst of laughter from the merchants coincided with a sharp crack from the largest mirror. Émilie turned to see a thin line appearing in the glass, like a black thread drawn across its surface. As she watched, the crack seemed to flex, widening for a moment to reveal something moving in the darkness beyond, before her mother smoothly positioned herself before it, blocking Émilie's view.

"The hour grows late," Madame de Sainte-Amaranthe announced, her voice carrying an authority that brooked no argument. "We must begin closing for the evening." The nobles rose immediately, gathering their winnings with unusual haste. The merchants protested but were expertly shepherded toward the door, their revolutionary fervor no match for her mother's practiced social grace.

As the salon emptied, Émilie caught a final glimpse of Charles. He was watching the cracked mirror, his hand still in his pocket, his reflection showing a grim determination that his actual face carefully concealed. Then her mother's hand was on her arm again, guiding her firmly away from both the mirror and whatever truth it might reveal.
